## 1. The question

**When a trial court adds substantive legal information to a response intended for a deliberating civil jury, does Code of Civil Procedure section 614 require notice to the parties of that additional information before the supplemented response is transmitted, or may compliance concerning the addition be supplied afterward or presumed from a record that does not establish notice of the completed response?**

Section 614 requires notice of the judicial addition before the supplemented response is transmitted. Compliance concerning the addition may not be supplied afterward. It may not be presumed from a record that does not establish notice of the completed response.

---

## 2. Section 614 governs the process of delivery

Section 614 governs the procedure by which judicial information reaches the deliberating jury. The statute requires that requested information be given "in the presence of, or after notice to, the parties or counsel." That protection attaches to the information given. When the given writing includes a judicial addition, notice of a different writing does not supply notice of the addition. A silent record on that point does not establish compliance.

---

## 3. Discretion over the content of the response

A trial court necessarily exercises judgment in determining how to respond when a deliberating jury asks a question of law. It may determine what law governs, whether clarification is necessary, how much explanation is appropriate, and how the response should be phrased. Those are questions concerning the substance of the court's response.

---

## 4. The statewide distinction

The distinction therefore matters statewide:

**Judicial discretion governs what lawful information the court chooses to provide. Section 614 governs the procedure by which that information reaches the deliberating jury.**

A court's discretion to decide what law to give the jury cannot include discretion to decide whether the parties receive the statutory opportunity to address the communication before it reaches the jury. Otherwise, the statutory safeguard becomes contingent upon the very judicial discretion it was enacted to regulate.

---

## 5. What the record proved

The material predicate is established: the court itself supplied additional legal information and communicated the response containing that information to the jury.

Extra legal language was added by the court. The supplemented response was sent to the deliberating jury. Those propositions rest on the court's own descriptions of what it did. They do not depend on an Evidence Code section 600(b) deduction.

The court described the street sentence as "gratuitous on the part of the Court" and stated that "the Court is defining." Rosario charged, "you sent that back." The court spoke of "delivering it to the Jury" and later asserted its "discretion to issue these responses." When confronted with the added language, the court defended its substance: "It says, 'Highway includes street.'" Counsel responded: "I have nothing to add, Your Honor, to what Your Honor said."

A separate judicial admission that "I violated section 614" is unnecessary. Section 614 regulates the act of supplying information to a deliberating jury. *Nelson v. Southern Pacific Co.* (1937) 8 Cal.2d 648, 655, applied the statute by examining what communication occurred and how it occurred.

Request No. 2 therefore supplies an independent section 614 issue. A showing of an earlier writing, if one occurred, is notice of that earlier writing. It does not, without more, establish notice of material language subsequently added by the court.

---

## 6. Communications that change the legal framework

The issue is especially important when the court does more than simply repeat an existing instruction. A jury communication may clarify existing law, add legal propositions, refuse requested guidance, combine several legal determinations into one response, or otherwise alter the legal framework within which deliberations continue. If the parties receive the completed substance only after it has been communicated, an objection may come too late to perform the function section 614 contemplates. The jury has already received the court's law.

---

## 7. Post-transmission notice does not cure the harm

Nor can post-transmission notice or a belated opportunity to object cure the harm. A deliberating jury's request for legal clarification marks a critical turning point in trial: the jurors have identified the exact legal pivot on which their verdict hangs. Unlike an ordinary instructional error buried among dozens of pre-deliberation instructions, a judge's mid-deliberation answer carries singular, targeted weight. Once an expanded, incomplete, or skewed legal framework reaches the jury room, it immediately reshapes deliberations and anchors juror positions. At that point, the bell cannot be unrung.

Even if a party discovers the added substance after transmission and asks the court to retract or modify what it just told the jury, a supplemental "correction" requires the court to walk back its own authority after deliberations have already crystallized around the initial answer. Section 614 requires notice before information is given precisely because pre-transmission input is the only mechanism capable of preventing that prejudice in the first place. Treating post-hoc notice, or pre-transmission assent to a narrower point, as sufficient strips section 614 of its preventive function at the exact moment in a civil trial when judicial communication is most influential.

---

## 8. Forfeiture and invited error

The same principle bears directly on forfeiture and invited error. A reviewing court should not treat assent to one proposition discussed before transmission as consent to a materially different or expanded judicial communication unless the record establishes that the party had notice of the challenged communication and assented to that communication itself. Otherwise, an incomplete opportunity to object can become the basis for holding that the objection was forfeited.

---

## 9. The circular result

That produces a circular result: the party loses the statutory opportunity to object before the communication reaches the jury, and then loses appellate review because the objection was supposedly not made or because an earlier statement concerning a different proposition is characterized as consent.

Section 614 should not operate that way.

If notice of an earlier writing automatically constitutes notice of a materially supplemented writing, then the protection of section 614 can disappear whenever a judge modifies a response after the parties have addressed an earlier version.

---

## 10. The rule

The rule needed for California civil trials is straightforward:

**When a deliberating jury requests information on a point of law, section 614 requires notice sufficient to provide the parties a meaningful opportunity to address the substantive communication the court proposes to give before that communication is delivered. When the record establishes that the court added legal information and transmitted the supplemented response, compliance as to that addition may not be presumed from silence. The record must establish notice of the judicial addition itself. The court retains discretion over the lawful substance of its response, but not discretion to eliminate the statutory procedure governing its delivery. Waiver, forfeiture, or invited error therefore must rest on conduct directed to the challenged communication itself, not merely on assent to a distinct proposition discussed during the process of formulating the response.**

This rule does not restrict legitimate trial-court discretion. It identifies where that discretion ends. It preserves the judge's authority to decide the law while preserving the Legislature's command concerning the manner in which new legal information is communicated to a deliberating jury.

---

## 11. Why the Court should grant review

The question accordingly extends far beyond the particular statutes, parties, and facts of this case. Every California civil trial court may receive a legal question from a deliberating jury. Trial judges, litigants, and reviewing courts need a uniform rule identifying when notice must occur and what information the parties must be permitted to address before a judicial communication reaches the deliberating jury.

The Court should grant review to settle that question.
