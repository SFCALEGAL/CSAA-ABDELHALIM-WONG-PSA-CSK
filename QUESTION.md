## 1. The question

**May a trial court's discretion over how to answer a deliberating jury's legal question extend to whether the parties receive the procedural protection required by Code of Civil Procedure section 614 before the court communicates its answer to the jury?**

It should not.

## 2. Discretion over the content of the response

A trial court necessarily exercises judgment in determining how to respond when a deliberating jury asks a question of law. It may determine what law governs, whether clarification is necessary, how much explanation is appropriate, and how the response should be phrased. Those are questions concerning the substance of the court's response.

## 3. Section 614 governs the process of delivery

Section 614 addresses something different. It governs the procedure by which judicial information reaches the deliberating jury. The statute requires that requested information be given "in the presence of, or after notice to, the parties or counsel." That protection would have little practical meaning if the same discretion that permits the court to formulate an answer also permitted the court to decide whether the parties would have a meaningful opportunity to address the answer before it reached the jury.

## 4. The statewide distinction

The distinction therefore matters statewide:

**Judicial discretion governs the content of the response. Section 614 governs the process by which the response reaches the jury.**

A court's discretion to decide what law to give the jury cannot include discretion to decide whether the parties receive the statutory opportunity to object to the communication before it reaches the jury. Otherwise, the statutory safeguard becomes contingent upon the very judicial discretion it was enacted to regulate.

## 5. Communications that change the legal framework

The issue is especially important when the court does more than simply repeat an existing instruction. A jury communication may clarify existing law, add legal propositions, refuse requested guidance, combine several legal determinations into one response, or otherwise alter the legal framework within which deliberations continue. If the parties receive the completed substance only after it has been communicated, an objection may come too late to perform the function section 614 contemplates. The jury has already received the court's law.

## 6. Forfeiture and invited error

The same principle bears directly on forfeiture and invited error. A reviewing court should not treat assent to one proposition discussed before transmission as consent to a materially different or expanded judicial communication unless the record establishes that the party had notice of the challenged communication and assented to that communication itself. Otherwise, an incomplete opportunity to object can become the basis for holding that the objection was forfeited.

## 7. The circular result

That produces a circular result: the party loses the statutory opportunity to object before the communication reaches the jury, and then loses appellate review because the objection was supposedly not made or because an earlier statement concerning a different proposition is characterized as consent.

Section 614 should not operate that way.

## 8. The rule

The rule needed for California civil trials is straightforward:

**When a deliberating jury requests information on a point of law, section 614 requires notice sufficient to provide the parties a meaningful opportunity to address the substantive communication the court proposes to give before that communication is delivered. The court retains discretion over the lawful substance of its response, but not discretion to eliminate the statutory procedure governing its delivery. Waiver, forfeiture, or invited error therefore must rest on conduct directed to the challenged communication itself, not merely on assent to a distinct proposition discussed during the process of formulating the response.**

This rule does not restrict legitimate trial-court discretion. It identifies where that discretion ends. It preserves the judge's authority to decide the law while preserving the Legislature's command concerning the manner in which new legal information is communicated to a deliberating jury.

## 9. Post-transmission notice does not cure the harm

Nor can post-transmission notice or a belated opportunity to object cure the harm. A deliberating jury's request for legal clarification marks a critical turning point in trial: the jurors have identified the exact legal pivot on which their verdict hangs. Unlike an ordinary instructional error buried among dozens of pre-deliberation instructions, a judge's mid-deliberation answer carries singular, targeted weight. Once an expanded, incomplete, or skewed legal framework reaches the jury room, it immediately reshapes deliberations and anchors juror positions. At that point, the bell cannot be unrung.

Even if a party discovers the added substance after transmission and asks the court to retract or modify what it just told the jury, a supplemental "correction" requires the court to walk back its own authority after deliberations have already crystallized around the initial answer. Section 614 requires notice before information is given precisely because pre-transmission input is the only mechanism capable of preventing that prejudice in the first place. Treating post-hoc notice, or pre-transmission assent to a narrower point, as sufficient strips section 614 of its preventive function at the exact moment in a civil trial when judicial communication is most influential.

## 10. Why the Court should grant review

The question accordingly extends far beyond the particular statutes, parties, and facts of this case. Every California civil trial court may receive a legal question from a deliberating jury. Trial judges, litigants, and reviewing courts need a uniform rule identifying what section 614 requires, when the parties must be heard, and what conduct can constitute waiver of that protection.

The Court should grant review to settle that question.
