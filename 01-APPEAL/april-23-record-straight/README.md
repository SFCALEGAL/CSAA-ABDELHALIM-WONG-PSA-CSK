## How to set the record straight

1. Name the writing. A jury form is one writing. A spoken sentence is another. A later sentence by the same speaker is another. Do not let one label cover two writings.
2. Open the pin. Volume 11 of the reporter's transcript, April 23, 2025 afternoon, or Super. Ct. doc. 09577565 (Jury Request Nos. 1 and 2).
3. Read the next sentence of the same speaker turn before treating the first sentence as a completed answer.
4. Assign the object. The words take the object they name. They do not, by themselves, take a later sentence spoken by the court.
5. Compare the postulation to that object. If the postulation moves the words onto a different act, the postulation is incorrect.

The pins below are the exclusive proof.

## Review 1. The first sentence of the jury request is the whole request

**A review might say:** Jury Request No. 1 asked only whether the jury should consider Vehicle Code sections 22107 and 21804(a). That is the entire request.

**A review might cite:** *Hurley v. Department of Parks & Recreation* (1969) 274 Cal.App.2d 61, 65 (a party must propose a complete instruction); Code of Civil Procedure section 647 (an order refusing an instruction is deemed excepted to).

**The record:** The same form continues: "If so could we be instructed on those provisions?" (Super. Ct. doc. 09577565, p. 2.)

**Why that reading is wrong.** A request for instruction is an affirmative writing. The second sentence is that writing. Section 647 names an order refusing an instruction. The form that contains the request also contains the court's later written answer. Treating the first sentence as the whole request deletes the writing section 647 names.

**Normalized definition.** Jury Request No. 1 is two sentences. Sentence one asks whether to consider the sections on negligence. Sentence two asks for instruction on those provisions if the answer is yes.

## Review 2. "I do agree with the first one" is consent to the whole written answer

**A review might say:** When Rosario said he agreed with "the first one," he approved the entire written response to Jury Request No. 1, including a later refusal to instruct.

**A review might cite:** *Eng v. Brown* (2018) 21 Cal.App.5th 675, 706 (invited error where a party persuades the court to adopt a ruling); *Fleming v. Safeco Ins. Co.* (1984) 160 Cal.App.3d 31, 43 (waiver by consent); *Mary M. v. City of Los Angeles* (1991) 54 Cal.3d 202, 212-213 (tactical choice).

**The record:** "I do agree with the first one, the response to number one: You may not consider it because it is not part of the instructions." (11 RT 4:8-10.) The next sentence of the same turn is: "But when you added that into the second one, it caused this, because the Jury had to make the decision based upon the available evidence that was there." (11 RT 4:11-14.)

**Why that reading is wrong.** The reported agreement names one object: a juror-use rule, and the reason he states is absence from the instructions. The next sentence assigns a different object to "the second one": the court's addition. *Eng*, *Fleming*, and *Mary M.* require an identified approval of the challenged act. The reported words identify the act he accepted. They do not identify the later spoken clause "nor will the Court instruct the Jury on those provisions." (11 RT 6:23-27.)

**Normalized definition.** The agreement at 11 RT 4:8-10 is a postulate about juror use. It is not a request for a statute. It is not approval of a refusal to instruct. A request is an affirmative act. The record at 11 RT 4:8-10 describes agreement to a use rule.

## Review 3. The word "No" is an unqualified acceptance

**A review might say:** Asked whether he had an objection to the instruction given in response to Jury Request No. 1, Rosario said "No," and that word closed the question.

**A review might cite:** *Huffman v. Interstate Brands Cos.* (2004) 121 Cal.App.4th 679, 705-707 (a qualified "I guess" is not a stipulation); *Stevens v. Owens-Corning Fiberglas Corp.* (1996) 49 Cal.App.4th 1645, 1653-1655 (invited error from a request); *Mary M.*, *supra*, 54 Cal.3d at pp. 212-213.

**The record:** The court asked: "So you don't have an objection to the instruction given in response to Jury request number one?" (11 RT 4:15-17.) The next speaker is one turn: "No. I have objection to the fact they were accessing or requesting all of them anyways." (11 RT 4:18-21.) The same turn continues through 11 RT 4:22-25: they should not have been accessing external information or requesting Vehicle Code sections that were not presented during the trial.

**Why that reading is wrong.** The word "No" is the first word of a continuous answer. The next grammatical sentence of that turn states the objection. *Huffman* reads a qualified answer as the whole answer. *Stevens* and *Mary M.* attach invited error to a request for the challenged act. This turn states an objection to access and request. It does not request the later refusal to instruct.

**Normalized definition.** 11 RT 4:18-25 is one speaker turn. The object of the objection is access and request. The "No" does not stand alone.

## Review 4. A later "I understand" or "that I agreed to" rewrites the afternoon pins

**A review might say:** Later lines in the same volume ("I understand," or a reference to what he had agreed to) convert the afternoon agreement into consent to both written replies, or into consent to the refusal clause.

**A review might cite:** *People v. Mora and Rangel* (2018) 5 Cal.5th 442, 498-499 (invited error where the defendant proposed the instruction); *Fleming*, *supra*, 160 Cal.App.3d at p. 43.

**The record:** After the court shows him that the Vehicle Code states a highway includes a street, he says "Understood." (11 RT 8:13-17.) That pin answers the statutory text just shown. On Request No. 3 he excludes the verdict-form clarification "as the defense wrote it, which he agreed to," and he preserves the mistrial for extemporaneous information accessed by the jury. (11 RT 9:9-17.) Those words identify Request No. 3 and the defense-written clarification.

**Why that reading is wrong.** Each later sentence has its own object. "Understood" at 11 RT 8:17 is an acknowledgment that the code contains the phrase the court just read. The agreement named at 11 RT 9:9-17 is the defense-written clarification on Request No. 3. Neither sentence recites the refusal clause at 11 RT 6:23-27. *Mora and Rangel* and *Fleming* require a proposal or consent aimed at the challenged instruction. These pins aim at different writings.

**Normalized definition.** A later acknowledgment takes the writing the court has just shown, or the request the speaker has just named. It does not travel backward onto a different clause.

## Review 5. The mistrial motion is the same act as approval of the replies

**A review might say:** Because Rosario moved for a mistrial about vehicle codes, he invited the court to keep those codes from the jury, or he waived any later objection to the written replies.

**A review might cite:** *People v. Bohana* (2000) 84 Cal.App.4th 360, 373 (invited error from a deliberate tactical choice); *Stevens*, *supra*, 49 Cal.App.4th at pp. 1653-1655; *Mary M.*, *supra*, 54 Cal.3d at pp. 212-213.

**The record:** Before the court recites "nor will the Court instruct," Rosario moves for a mistrial: one or more jurors accessed or relied on outside information, specifically vehicle codes, which is immaterial, which was not introduced into evidence. (11 RT 3:12-22.) He submits that motion. (11 RT 6:12.) He later objects that the court enhanced Reply No. 2 and sent it back. (11 RT 8:7-12.) He tells the court, "That wasn't our understanding," when the court says no objection was raised when the answers were completed. (11 RT 10:9-12.) The court itself describes what he submitted as an objection to the court's responses to forms one and two, already considered and overruled. (11 RT 12:27-13:6.)

**Why that reading is wrong.** A mistrial motion is a charge about juror use and a charge about the court's sending. It is not a request for the court's later refusal sentence. *Bohana*, *Stevens*, and *Mary M.* attach invited error to a request for the ruling under review. The mistrial pin names access, immaterial vehicle codes, and later the enhancement the court sent. The court's own later description treats the submission as an objection to the two responses. An objection to an act is a position on that act.

**Normalized definition.** The mistrial at 11 RT 3:12-22 takes the jury's use of vehicle-code information that was not evidence. "Immaterial" in that sentence sits on those vehicle codes. Submission at 11 RT 6:12 is not agreement to either reply.

## Review 6. "Immaterial" is a concession that the statutes do not matter

**A review might say:** Rosario called the vehicle codes immaterial, so he conceded they had no place in the charge, and a later refusal to instruct followed from his own word.

**A review might cite:** *Hurley*, *supra*, 274 Cal.App.2d at p. 65.

**The record:** "Immaterial" appears in the mistrial sentence: vehicle codes "which is immaterial, which was not introduced into evidence." (11 RT 3:12-22.) The same afternoon he objects to the court's enhancement of Reply No. 2 and to the sending of that text. (11 RT 8:7-12.)

**Why that reading is wrong.** The adjective sits on the vehicle codes as outside information in a mistrial charge. It does not recast Jury Request No. 1's second sentence. It does not approve 11 RT 6:23-27. A statute given as law is a question of law. (Evid. Code, § 310, subd. (a).)

**Normalized definition.** "Immaterial" at 11 RT 3:12-22 is a label on vehicle-code information used as if it were evidence. It is not a request to omit instruction. It is not a concession that the jury's second sentence was idle.

## Review 7. Code of Civil Procedure section 614 never attached, because someone was present

**A review might say:** Section 614 is only about notice and presence. Counsel and the plaintiff were in the courtroom for the afternoon hearing, so the statute has no further work, and any later sending was cured.

**A review might cite:** Code of Civil Procedure section 614; *Flores v. Liu* (2021) 60 Cal.App.5th 278, 289 (information given a deliberating jury must be written, or given in open court, after notice).

**The record:** Replies No. 1 and No. 2 are already time-stamped at 10:37 a.m. and 10:46 a.m., before the afternoon hearing. (Super. Ct. doc. 09577565.) Rosario alleges he objected to material going back when the reporter was not present, and he asks that the record be corrected. (11 RT 10:23-11:22.) The court states it added "'Highway' includes street" and sent that sentence, and it calls the addition gratuitous. (11 RT 7:16-8:6.) Rosario answers that the court enhanced the statement and sent it back. (11 RT 8:7-12.)

**Why that reading is wrong.** Section 614 is a duty of method: legal information given to a deliberating jury must be in writing, or stated in open court, after notice to counsel. *Flores* states that duty. Presence at a later hearing does not rewrite a form already stamped in the morning. Presence does not convert a gratuitous addition into notice of the refusal clause. The afternoon pins still assign each sentence to its own object.

**Normalized definition.** Section 614 is the method of transmission. Notice and a writing, or an open-court statement after notice, are the method. A later hearing is a later hearing.

## Review 8. The court's reason proves the jury was researching, so the statutes stay out

**A review might say:** The jury asked about sections 22107 and 21804(a). That ask is proof of outside research. The proper answer is therefore a prohibition on the sections, and a refusal to instruct.

**A review might cite:** *Sesler v. Ghumman* (1990) 219 Cal.App.3d 218, 227 (a turning motorist must signal and yield); Evidence Code section 310, subdivision (a) (questions of law are for the court).

**The record:** The court's own stated ground is: there is no evidence the jury considered those sections, "and those Vehicle Code sections were not part of the evidence in this case." (11 RT 7:4-9.) The same afternoon the court sends other Vehicle Code text to the same jury and calls one added sentence gratuitous. (11 RT 7:16-8:6.)

**Why that reading is wrong.** The first clause of the court's ground is a finding that outside consideration was not proved. A review that treats the jury's written question as proved research contradicts that clause. The second clause treats the named sections as if a statute were an exhibit. Evidence Code section 310, subdivision (a), assigns statutes to the court as questions of law. *Sesler* states a duty that lives in the Vehicle Code. The court's own sending of other Vehicle Code text the same afternoon is the court's own legal information, not a juror's private book.

**Normalized definition.** The court's ground at 11 RT 7:4-9 has two clauses. Clause one: no evidence of consideration. Clause two: the sections were not part of the evidence. Clause one is a sufficiency finding on research. Clause two is a classification of a statute as if it were an exhibit.

## Review 9. Overruled objections and a later "Right" erase the afternoon objections

**A review might say:** Once the court said the objections were considered and overruled, and Rosario said "Right," the objections no longer exist as objections, or they became consent.

**A review might cite:** *Fleming*, *supra*, 160 Cal.App.3d at p. 43; Code of Civil Procedure section 647.

**The record:** The clerk reports that he wants objections to form number one and form number two on the record. He answers "Yes." (11 RT 10:4-8.) He then says, "That wasn't our understanding," when the court states that no objection was raised when the answers were completed. (11 RT 10:9-12.) The court later describes the submission as an objection to the two responses, already considered and overruled. He answers "Right." (11 RT 12:27-13:6.) He then states he understands the denial of the mistrial and does not agree. (11 RT 13:26-14:5.)

**Why that reading is wrong.** Section 647 deems an order overruling an objection excepted to. An overruled objection remains an objection. "Right" at 11 RT 13:6 answers the court's description of what he submitted. It is not a withdrawal. "That wasn't our understanding" at 11 RT 10:12 contradicts the claim that the completed answers drew no objection.

**Normalized definition.** An overruled objection is an objection the court has ruled on. The exception is statutory. Agreement to the court's description of the submission is not approval of the replies.

## Review 10. The street sentence is harmless surplus, so the sending does not matter

**A review might say:** Adding "'Highway' includes street" was unnecessary but harmless. Calling it gratuitous ends the point. Sending Vehicle Code text after a prohibition on other sections is housekeeping.

**A review might cite:** *Soule v. General Motors Corp.* (1994) 8 Cal.4th 548, 573-580 (instructional error is reviewed for prejudice under article VI, section 13).

**The record:** The court states it is adding the sentence and sending it, and it calls the addition gratuitous. (11 RT 7:16-8:6.) Rosario answers that the court enhanced the statement and sent it back, and that the sending is a direct influence over the jury. (11 RT 8:7-12.) The same volume already contains the court's prohibition-and-refusal sentence on sections 22107 and 21804(a). (11 RT 6:23-27.)

**Why that reading is wrong.** *Soule* requires evaluation of prejudice. It does not rewrite the verbs on the page. The verbs on 11 RT 8:7-12 are enhanced and sent. The court's own word for the added sentence is gratuitous. Those are record facts about transmission. They remain available for the evaluation *Soule* describes.

**Normalized definition.** Reply No. 2, as sent, includes a sentence the court added and labeled gratuitous. Rosario's object at 11 RT 8:7-12 is that addition and that sending.

## What the afternoon pins prove, and what they do not

A person who opens volume 11 RT and Super. Ct. doc. 09577565 can prove each of the following without any reviewing court's narrative:

- The jury asked two questions on one form: whether to consider sections 22107 and 21804(a), and whether, if so, the jury could be instructed on those provisions. (Super. Ct. doc. 09577565, p. 2.)
- Rosario's agreement names a juror-use rule and a reason: absence from the instructions. (11 RT 4:8-10.)
- The next sentence of that turn names the court's addition. (11 RT 4:11-14.)
- The answer to the court's follow-up question is a continuous objection to access and request. (11 RT 4:15-25.)
- The court's later sentence, "nor will the Court instruct the Jury on those provisions," is a different writing, spoken by the court. (11 RT 6:23-27.)
- The court's stated ground is the absence of evidence that the jury considered the sections, and the classification of those sections as outside the evidence. (11 RT 7:4-9.)
- The same afternoon the court sent other Vehicle Code text and called one sentence gratuitous. (11 RT 7:16-8:6.)
- Rosario objected to that enhancement and sending. (11 RT 8:7-12.)
- He placed objections to both completed forms on the record, and he rejected the claim that the completed answers drew no objection. (11 RT 10:4-12.)
- The court treated the submission as an objection to both responses and overruled it. (11 RT 12:27-13:6.)

Those propositions are exclusive to the cited lines. A postulation that moves "I do agree with the first one," "No," "immaterial," "Understood," or "Right" onto the refusal clause, or onto both written replies as a single consented package, is incorrect on those lines.

The authorities listed above (*Hurley*, *Flores*, *Eng*, *Sesler*, *Fleming*, *Bohana*, *Mary M.*, *Stevens*, *Mora and Rangel*, *Huffman*, *Soule*; Code Civ. Proc., §§ 614, 647; Evid. Code, § 310) are published law a review might apply. Each of them still requires an identified act. The April 23 pins identify the act. They do not identify the act the postulation needs.
