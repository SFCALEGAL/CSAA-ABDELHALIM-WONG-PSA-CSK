And there is another distinction that deserves your attention: *there is a fundamental difference between vexatious litigation and the diligent pursuit of justice.* A litigant who persistently identifies errors, challenges mischaracterizations, seeks correction of the judicial record, and exercises available appellate remedies is not thereby engaging in vexatious conduct. Persistence in pursuing legal accountability is not synonymous with abuse of the judicial process.

I am beginning to question whether you recognize that distinction. Your apparent difficulty separating diligent pursuit of judicial remedies from supposedly vexatious conduct raises concerns about how you characterize legitimate challenges to your client's position. *Calling a litigant vexatious does not make the underlying errors disappear, nor does it relieve opposing counsel of the obligation to address the actual record.*

There is a meaningful difference between defending a judgment on its merits and attempting to discredit the person who continues to question how that judgment was obtained.

## The Anatomy of an Appellate Ambush: How the Defense Turned an Objection Into Consent and Won Without Reaching the Merits

The most elegant way to defeat an appeal is not to prove that the trial judge was right. It is to persuade the appellate court that the injured plaintiff agreed to the very error he is challenging. Once that proposition is accepted, the court can avoid examining the underlying mistake altogether. That is precisely what happened in *Rosario v. Abdelhalim*, A173827, where the defense constructed an appellate narrative from carefully selected transcript passages, reassigned the objects of Rosario's statements, and secured a September 10, 2026 opinion that stopped the appeal before the merits. The strategy was sophisticated, economical, and devastatingly effective. Its fatal weakness, however, is that the complete record tells a materially different story.

The case arose from a pedestrian collision in which Rosario maintained that Abdelhalim struck him while he was walking his scooter through a marked crosswalk. Abdelhalim acknowledged making a right turn and, in a contemporaneous 911 recording, admitted striking Rosario, although he later denied contact. An eyewitness also identified the van's driver as having struck the person on the ground. Yet the defense obtained a nine-to-three jury verdict on negligence. The appeal concerned, among other issues, the court's failure to instruct on statutory duties governing the driver's turn and its subsequent refusal to supply those instructions when the jury expressly requested them. Rather than confront whether that refusal was legally correct, the defense persuaded the Court of Appeal that Rosario had consented to it.

The first revealing omission appears in the appellate opinion's treatment of the jury's own written request. The jurors asked: "In considering the question of whether the defendant was negligent should we consider the provisions of Vehicle Code Sections 22107 and 21804(a). If so could we be instructed on those provisions?" (Super. Ct. doc. 09577565, p. 2.) The second sentence is crucial. These jurors were asking their judge to provide legal instructions on statutory duties potentially governing the defendant's conduct. The defense's August 3, 2026 brief actually reproduced the complete request, but then characterized the ensuing dispute as Rosario's opposition to the statutes. The Court of Appeal's opinion quoted only the first sentence, omitting the express request for instruction. A jury seeking judicial clarification was thus portrayed through an incomplete question, making it easier to describe Rosario's subsequent objections as opposition to any further instruction. *The missing sentence identified the very judicial duty the opinion avoided examining.*

The trial judge's completed response contained two legally different propositions: "You may not consider Vehicle Code Sections 22107 and or 21804 subdivision A, nor will the Court instruct the Jury on those provisions." (11 RT 6:23-27.) The first clause prohibited independent consideration of statutes absent from the jury charge. The second affirmatively refused to provide the requested legal instructions. The defense's theory depended on treating those propositions as interchangeable. They are not. Agreement that jurors cannot independently apply uninstructed law does not constitute agreement that the judge should refuse to instruct them on potentially applicable law. The defense's central maneuver was to take consent directed toward the first proposition and attach it to the second.

The most consequential example involves Rosario's explanation of what he understood to be the original response discussed during an earlier, unreported conference. Rosario and members of his legal assistance team maintain that the first version addressed the prohibition against independent juror consideration of uninstructed statutes. Rosario later reaffirmed that limited understanding: "I do agree with the first one, the response to number one: You may not consider it because it is not part of the instructions." (11 RT 4:8-10.) *The object of that agreement was the original restriction governing juror conduct, not the additional refusal to instruct contained in the completed writing.* Nevertheless, defense counsel repeatedly isolated those words and presented them as proof that Rosario agreed the statutes should be withheld altogether.

Even more revealing is what immediately followed. Rosario was distinguishing the original understanding from what he contended the judge had subsequently added to the completed response. He continued: "But when you added that into the second one, it caused this, because the Jury had to make the decision based upon the available evidence that was there." (11 RT 4:11-14.) *Rosario identifies "the second one" in this exchange as the materially changed second version of Reply No. 1, which he says added the court's affirmative refusal to instruct.* Judge Wong's immediate follow-up question concerned Reply No. 1, and the judge subsequently read the completed refusal into the record. By ending its preferred quotation before this qualification, the defense removed the very language that identified a disputed judicial addition. What remained was a seemingly unconditional agreement, stripped of the context needed to determine what had actually been accepted.

The next exchange supplied another opportunity to reverse the object of Rosario's statement. When Judge Wong asked whether he objected to the response to Jury Request No. 1, Rosario addressed the predicament created by the missing instructions: "No. I have objection to the fact they were accessing or requesting all of them anyways." (11 RT 4:18-20.) *His objection concerned the jury having been left without the relevant statutory guidance and consequently finding itself in the position of requesting it during deliberations.* The defense treated the opening "No" and the reference to jurors requesting statutes as proof that Rosario opposed providing those instructions. But objecting to the necessity of a request is not objecting to granting the request. The distinction is fundamental: *an objection to the failure to provide something was transformed into consent to continue withholding it.*

The defense amplified this interpretation in its written advocacy. Its opening brief asserted that it was "undisputable that Appellant vehemently opposed giving any instruction on Vehicle Code section 22107." This was an extraordinary expansion of the actual record. Rosario's reported mistrial motion concerned suspected independent juror access to outside Vehicle Code material, while his other objections challenged additional statutory information introduced by the judge himself. Neither subject is identical to the judicial refusal to provide section 22107 when the jury requested instructions. Yet the defense combined them into a single narrative of opposition, invited error, and waiver. The tactic converted several distinct objections into evidence supposedly proving that Rosario induced the result he was appealing.

That distortion becomes clearer when the two jury responses are examined separately. Request No. 1 concerned the defendant's statutory turning and yielding duties. Request No. 2 concerned the definition of a vehicle, an issue supporting the defense's attempt to characterize Rosario as operating a scooter rather than walking as a pedestrian. Judge Wong supplied a vehicle definition and additional language from Vehicle Code section 360 explaining that "Highway includes street." Rosario objected to the additional definitional material. The judge described the supplementation as potentially "gratuitous" but legally accurate. Section 360 does contain the quoted language; its correctness, however, answers a different question from whether the judge materially changed the proposed response and transmitted the completed revision without prior party review and an opportunity to object before jury delivery.

Rosario and members of his legal assistance team maintain that they reviewed earlier versions of both responses during the unreported conference, but Judge Wong subsequently made material additions: the refusal to instruct in Reply No. 1 and the highway/street language in Reply No. 2. They state that the completed versions were delivered to the jury without their having first inspected the changes or having an opportunity to object. When the team later examined the completed writings, its members identified the differences and notified Rosario. The reporter's transcript independently records the completed language and Rosario's subsequent objections; the earlier discussions and delivery sequence are matters supported by Rosario's and his team's firsthand account. Those circumstances present a concrete factual dispute about the scope of any agreement. They cannot simply be resolved by assuming that approval of an earlier version constituted approval of every subsequent addition.

Even Rosario's reference to "common knowledge" was reassigned. In the reported proceeding, his objection concerned the necessity of supplying an additional definition of a vehicle, which he considered unnecessary. The defense's broader account treated that position as part of his supposed opposition to the turning-duty instructions. But an objection to supplementing a definition that could support the defense's scooter theory is entirely consistent with an objection to withholding the law governing the driver's turn. *The contradiction was manufactured by merging two different subjects and then treating the resulting confusion as proof of waiver.*

The defense also exploited the absence of a transcript from the earlier conference. Its September 1 opposition argued that the missing record of the instruction-selection discussions undermined preservation of the appellate claims, while suggesting that the parties might simply have agreed on the original charge. But the reported proceedings themselves contain Rosario's disagreement with the judge's characterization of earlier acquiescence and his request to reconstruct the unreported events. The court had also read the original instructions out of numerical order and promised a numbered written copy after the jury retired; Rosario and his legal team maintain that they never received that completed charge before the jury's request exposed the omissions. *The absence of a record proving an earlier objection cannot itself become affirmative evidence of an earlier agreement.* Where the identity of the agreed document and the timing of its revision determine the outcome, California Rules of Court, rule 8.155(c)(2), provides a mechanism for settling material disputes concerning the record.

The strategy culminated in the appellate opinion's conclusion that Rosario "forfeited his claim by agreeing to the trial court's response to the jury's question." That finding functioned as a procedural stop. Once the Court of Appeal accepted the supposed agreement, it had no occasion to determine whether the trial judge complied with Code of Civil Procedure section 614, whether the statutory turning-duty instruction was required, whether the refusal was erroneous, or whether the jury's express request and nine-to-three negligence verdict demonstrated prejudice. The defense had achieved the ideal procedural victory: affirmation without meaningful adjudication of the challenged instructional refusal.

California Supreme Court jurisprudence exposes the weakness in that stopping point. *Norgart v. Upjohn Co.* (1999) 21 Cal.4th 383 requires invited error to rest on conduct inducing the particular judicial error challenged. *Mary M. v. City of Los Angeles* (1991) 54 Cal.3d 202 distinguishes inducement from acquiescence in an adverse ruling. Code of Civil Procedure section 647 deems refusals to give instructions excepted to, while *Cook v. Los Angeles Railway Corp.* (1939) 13 Cal.2d 591 and *Lund v. San Joaquin Valley Railroad* (2003) 31 Cal.4th 1 reinforce the importance of statutory preservation rules. Section 647 does not excuse genuine invited error, but neither does a failure to repeat an objection prove intentional consent. The court must identify the actual judicial act the litigant induced or approved. Agreement with a preexisting rule against independent juror research is not, by itself, inducement of the court's later refusal to instruct.

The underlying merits also remain significant. Section 614 governs requests for legal guidance from a deliberating jury. The judge refused the turning-duty provisions in part because they were not part of the evidence, although statutes are law rather than evidentiary exhibits. Meanwhile, the court provided supplementary Vehicle Code definitions relevant to the defense's theory. Under *Soule v. General Motors Corp.* (1994) 8 Cal.4th 548, determining prejudice from instructional error requires consideration of the evidence, the other instructions, counsel's arguments, and indications of jury confusion. The jurors' express request was particularly relevant to that inquiry. The appellate court never reached it because the defense's consent theory ended the case first.

Our examination exposed the mechanism by restoring the missing context and assigning each statement its actual object. The jury requested instructions; the judge refused them. Rosario acknowledged a restriction on independent juror consideration; the defense converted that acknowledgment into approval of the refusal. Rosario objected to the jury's need to seek missing law; the defense described that as opposition to receiving the law. Rosario challenged supplemental vehicle definitions; the defense treated the challenge as rejection of the driver's turning duties. The court characterized an unreported discussion as acquiescence; Rosario disputed that characterization and sought to reconstruct it. *The decisive error was not necessarily a fabricated quotation. It was the relocation of genuine words from the subjects they addressed to judicial acts they did not necessarily approve.*

This criticism concerns the arguments and representations contained in public appellate filings, not an established finding about counsel's private intentions. The record permits a forceful conclusion about the vulnerability of the defense's reasoning without claiming to know why counsel framed it that way. The technique was effective precisely because authentic transcript excerpts gave the resulting narrative an appearance of documentary authority.

Rosario has now presented these concerns through rehearing proceedings and his petition for California Supreme Court review in S299144. Review remains discretionary, and reversal is not guaranteed. But if the Supreme Court examines the legal basis of the procedural stop, it must confront the distinction on which the defense's victory depends: *what Rosario actually agreed to, which version of the response he reviewed, and whether that agreement induced the specific refusal now attributed to him.* If that connection fails, the consent-based basis for stopping the appeal fails with it, requiring consideration of the remaining legal issues through the appropriate review process.

The defense's appellate achievement was undeniably elegant: it transformed a complicated instructional-error case into a seemingly simple forfeiture decision. But elegance cannot substitute for fidelity to the record. A quotation is not proof of waiver until its subject is correctly identified. An objection does not become consent because opposing counsel assigns it a different purpose. And an opinion cannot gain a sound foundation merely by stopping before the omitted context becomes inconvenient. *The defense won by persuading the appellate court to accept a particular meaning of the record. The challenge now is to show, from that same record, why the meaning assigned to Rosario's words cannot sustain the judgment.*
