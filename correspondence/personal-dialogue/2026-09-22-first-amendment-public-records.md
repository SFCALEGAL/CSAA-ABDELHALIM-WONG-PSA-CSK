## Sent text

Counsel,

I understand that the defense would apparently prefer that I stop publicly
discussing information contained in public records, including statements
and admissions made on the record concerning corporate conduct that I
believe bears directly upon matters of public concern and public safety.

There is, however, a rather significant obstacle to that position: the
First Amendment.

I am entitled to read public records. I am entitled to discuss public
records. I am entitled to publish my opinions and commentary concerning
those records. And where those records document corporate practices,
statements, admissions, or conduct that bear upon matters of legitimate
public concern or public safety, the Constitution does not transform my
publication of that information into misconduct merely because the
corporation or its attorneys would prefer that fewer people know about it.

That is rather the point of freedom of speech.

The First Amendment would provide remarkably little protection if a person
could speak about corporate conduct only until the corporation being
criticized found the speech inconvenient.

The defense now appears to be attempting something even more extraordinary:
taking my public commentary concerning publicly available records and
corporate conduct and repackaging that protected expression as evidence
that I should be declared a vexatious litigant.

Speech is not litigation.

Publishing commentary about a public court record does not become the
filing of a lawsuit. Reporting what a corporation or its representatives
stated or admitted on the record does not become litigation merely because
the corporation dislikes having those statements repeated. Warning the
public about conduct that the speaker reasonably believes affects consumers
or public safety does not become an abuse of the judicial process merely
because the warning is critical.

If that were the constitutional standard, journalists, consumer advocates,
whistleblowers, watchdog organizations, former employees, litigants, and
ordinary citizens could all be silenced by the remarkably simple expedient
of characterizing their criticism as evidence of supposedly improper
conduct.

Fortunately, the First Amendment is not quite that fragile.

The constitutional protection afforded to discussion of matters of public
concern is especially important where the information being discussed comes
from records already available to the public. Once information has been
placed into the public domain through official proceedings and public
records, there is an obvious and substantial First Amendment interest in
permitting citizens to examine it, discuss it, criticize it, and bring it
to the attention of others.

I therefore have no intention of accepting the defense's premise that my
exercise of protected speech somehow becomes evidence of vexatious
litigation simply because my commentary concerns this corporation, its
attorneys, or conduct revealed in public records.

If the defense believes I satisfy California's statutory definition of a
vexatious litigant, then it should prove the statutory elements based upon
the litigation conduct identified by the Legislature.

But my public commentary is something different.

It is speech.

And there is something particularly troubling about asking a court to
restrict a citizen's access to the judicial system while pointing to that
citizen's public criticism of corporate conduct as a reason why the
restriction should be imposed. The Constitution protects both the right to
speak on matters of public concern and the right to petition the government
for redress. Attempting to turn the exercise of one constitutional right
into evidence supporting the restriction of another deserves careful
constitutional scrutiny.

The defense is certainly free to disagree with my commentary. It may
respond publicly. It may dispute my interpretation of the records. It may
identify any factual statement it believes is inaccurate.

What it should not do is ask the Court to treat constitutionally protected
public commentary as though it were itself an abusive lawsuit.

A public record does not become private because its contents are
uncomfortable.

An admission made on the record does not become confidential because
repeating it is inconvenient.

And criticism of corporate conduct does not cease to be speech because the
corporation would rather not be criticized.

That is precisely why the First Amendment exists.

And there you go.

Regards,

Frank Rosario
